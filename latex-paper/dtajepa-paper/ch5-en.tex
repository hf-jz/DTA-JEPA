\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\noindent Every number below is produced by \texttt{dtajepa.evaluate} from a single command
per suite and aggregated by \texttt{dtajepa.report}; the tables included here
(\texttt{results-tables.tex}) are generated files, not transcribed numbers. Raw rows are in
\texttt{results/raw.jsonl} (114 runs), \texttt{results/ablation.jsonl} (60) and
\texttt{results/meta.jsonl} (14).

\subsection{What the comparison can and cannot resolve}

The sweep evaluates 38 (condition, seed) pairs $\times$ 3 methods, 12 episodes per run, at MPC
horizon 8, $\le 6$ replans, 10 GD steps and a 15-step goal. With 12 episodes the per-condition
resolution is 8.3 percentage points, and the spread across conditions is large (s.d.\ $\approx
22$ points). Everything below is reported with that granularity in mind: differences under
roughly 10 points per family are not distinguishable here. This is a \emph{screening}
experiment, and we flag explicitly which conclusions the data supports.

\textbf{The honest summary is that the three methods are statistically indistinguishable on
this budget.} Combined over all conditions the three means lie within $1.9$ points of each
other against a per-condition spread of $21$--$22$ points. Test-time adaptation (AdaJEPA) does
not separate from the frozen model either, and neither does our controller separate from
AdaJEPA. On the maze families the frozen model is the strongest (layout $70.0$ vs
$65.8/68.3$), which matches AdaJEPA's own observation that a well-trained model on an easy
navigation task leaves little room for adaptation.

Two things we did \emph{not} find, stated plainly because they are the claims the method was
built to make: (i) no gain from uncertainty allocation over a fixed one-step update at equal
budget on the manipulation families; (ii) no gain from the FOMAML initialisation (shape $32.7$
with the plain initialisation vs $32.1$ with the meta-learned one, 14 runs each).

\subsection{The visual family is a no-op for any latent planner}\label{sec:noop}

The eight visual conditions produce \emph{numerically identical} per-condition results for each
method (e.g.\ AdaJEPA $25.0\%$ on every one of default, blur, blur-strong, snp, dark, red
agent, red block, red anchor). The reason is not encoder robustness; it is that the planner
never sees the corruption individually. The MPC cost is a \emph{difference} of latents,
$\norm{\yhat_k - z_g}^2$, and every photometric corruption we apply is applied to both the
current frame and the goal frame. \S\ref{sec:calibres} quantifies it: blur at level 0.9 moves
the one-step latent prediction error from $25.80$ to $25.79$ ($0.02\%$).

This is a property of latent-distance planning rather than of our model. A consistent
photometric transform that approximately preserves the relative geometry of the embedding
leaves goal-conditioned planning unchanged, while colour shifts that remove the visual
distinction between the moving object and the static reference can change it (colour conditions
are the only visual conditions that differ at all in our data, and only by one episode).
Strengthening the corruption does not help: strong blur (level 1.8) is identical to standard
blur. AdaJEPA reports real degradation under these same corruptions; the difference is that
their encoder is a frozen foundation model whose feature geometry is not invariant to the
corruption, whereas ours is trained end-to-end on the same rendering pipeline as the
corruptions.

The consequence for the comparison is that the visual family is effectively 12 episodes
repeated eight times, so DTA-JEPA's apparent $+15.6$-point advantage there is a difference of
two episodes out of twelve --- i.e.\ noise.

\subsection{Uncertainty diagnostics}\label{sec:calibres}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Uncertainty diagnostics on the PushBlock model. \emph{mean var} is the predicted
variance, \emph{mean err} the realised squared latent error.}
\label{tab:calib}
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
Stream & mean err & mean var & Pearson & Spearman & relative ECE \\
\midrule
in distribution & 25.80 & 22.81 & 0.38 & 0.55 & 82\% \\
unseen shapes   & 21.69 & 21.72 & 0.45 & 0.64 & 79\% \\
blurred ($\ell=0.9$) & 25.79 & 22.80 & 0.38 & 0.55 & 82\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three findings, each of which constrains the design.

\paragraph{The variance head is rank-informative but scale-compressed.} Spearman rank
correlation between predicted variance and realised error is $0.55$ in distribution and $0.64$
on unseen shapes, while the reliability curve spans $2.7$--$6.8$ in predicted variance against
$11.7$--$52.0$ in realised error: the ordering carries information, the dynamic range does not.
The relative calibration error is $82\%$. This justifies the choice our controller makes ---
allocate on the \emph{ratio} of surprise to its running mean rather than on an absolute
threshold --- and warns against threshold-based OOD detection built on this head.

\paragraph{Recursive refinement saturates at depth three.} Mean squared latent error per
refinement depth is $42.5 \to 28.8 \to 24.6 \to 25.8$ in distribution and $40.5 \to 25.4 \to
20.6 \to 21.7$ on unseen shapes, measured in one forward pass with supervision at every depth.
This is the clearest positive result in the paper: \emph{two extra refinement steps recover
$42\%$ of the one-step error}, and the fourth step is past the useful point --- it slightly
increases error. It motivates both halves of the design: recursion with deep supervision is
worth having, and adaptive halting is not optional, because spending the fourth step hurts.

One qualification, from the navigation model (\S\ref{app:maze}): under the low-mass dynamics
shift refinement makes the error \emph{worse at every depth} ($97.3 \to 130.5$). The recursion
operator was trained under default dynamics, so iterating it amplifies the error it encodes.
Recursion is an accuracy knob when the shift is in the input distribution and a liability when
the shift is in the operator itself --- which is exactly the case where adaptation, not extra
iterations, is the right currency.

\paragraph{Cross-stream surprise is not comparable.} Mean in-distribution error is
\emph{higher} than mean unseen-shape error ($25.8$ vs $21.7$). The confound is episode
difficulty: the held-out geometries are smaller (square, I, small-T) and thus easier to predict,
so comparing raw surprise across suites does not measure shift. Within a stream the ordering
holds ($\rho = 0.55$--$0.64$). Any deployment that uses surprise to compare \emph{different}
environments must normalise it, which is what a ratio-based controller does.

\subsection{Adaptation statistics: the anchor binds and allocation saturates}

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{DTA-JEPA online statistics per episode (means over all main-sweep runs).}
\label{tab:stats}
\begin{tabular}{lcl}
\toprule
Quantity & Value & Interpretation \\
\midrule
gradient steps per update & 2.2 & inside the intended range $[1,3]$ \\
learning rate per update & $2.0\times10^{-3}$ & $4\times$ base rate: allocation saturates high \\
memories retrieved per episode & 13.4 & one retrieval per update \\
anchor projections per episode & 159 & $\approx$ every gradient step \\
rollbacks per episode & 0.2 & validation gate rarely fires \\
skill-bank entries & 0 & not exercised in parallel evaluation (\S\ref{sec:limits}) \\
planning / adaptation time (s) & 33 / 6 & adaptation is $13\%$ of planning (AdaJEPA: $5\%$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two of these numbers explain the null result.

\textbf{The anchor ball, not the learning rate, sets the step size.} With $594.7$k fast
parameters and Adam, a single step of norm $\eta\sqrt{n} \approx 5\times10^{-4}\cdot 770$
exceeds $\epsilon_f = 0.8$ immediately, so the projection fires on essentially every gradient
step ($159$ per episode). At $\epsilon_f=0.8$ the DTA-JEPA configuration is therefore not
"AdaJEPA plus a controller"; it is "AdaJEPA plus a hard trust region", and the ablation in
which the safety layer is removed gives the highest shape-family number of all ablation rows
($35.7$ vs $29.8$), consistent with the constraint being what costs accuracy.

\textbf{Allocation saturates.} The controller allocates a mean learning-rate multiplier of
$4\times$ and 2.2 steps per update --- close to its upper limit --- because predicted variance
is systematically compressed, so surprise divided by its running mean stays above one. In this
regime the controller is not selecting cheap in-distribution updates; it is applying a
uniformly stronger update, which is the failure mode the design was meant to avoid.

\subsection{Parity runs: the paper's own example set, 30 steps out}\label{sec:parity}

To make the comparison observable where AdaJEPA's figures are readable, we re-ran the shape
suite with their 30-step x-axis, their three adaptation knobs, and their second planner.

\textbf{Thirty replanning steps.} Mean final success over the seven shapes (6 episodes per
condition, GD planner): Frozen $31.0$, AdaJEPA $43.0$, DTA-JEPA $33.4$. This reproduces the
\emph{direction} of AdaJEPA's headline result --- test-time adaptation does help over a frozen
model when the executor is given a longer budget --- and it also reproduces the ordering of our
main sweep, with our controller behind their fixed one-step update. The per-shape table is on
the project page; with 6 episodes per condition the granularity is $16.7$ points, so only the
aggregate is worth reading.

\textbf{The knobs barely move either method (their Fig.~9).} Sweeping the test-time learning
rate ($0.2$--$5\times$), the number of adaptation steps ($1,2,5$) and the replay buffer
($1,5,20$) over the same suite gives AdaJEPA $31.0$--$38.1\%$ and DTA-JEPA $33.3$--$42.9\%$
--- a 7--9-point spread against a $\pm 7.5$-point sampling error, with no monotone trend in any
knob. This agrees with AdaJEPA's own conclusion that a single step at the training learning
rate is a strong default, and it is why we did not tune our method into a win: at this budget
the adaptation hyper-parameters are not the binding constraint. (DTA-JEPA's only high point,
$42.9\%$, is at the $5\times$ learning rate --- i.e.\ at the point where the anchor projection
in \S\ref{sec:calibres} stops being able to pull the update back.)

\textbf{Planner independence.} CEM (64 candidates, 5 iterations, 4 episodes, 4 replans)
reproduces the same non-separation: the three methods differ by at most one episode on every
shape, and CEM is $8\times$ cheaper than our GD configuration in wall-clock terms, so the null
result is not an artefact of the optimiser.

\subsection{Ablations and meta-initialisation are underpowered}

The ablation rows ($7$ shape conditions $\times 12$ episodes $= 84$ episodes per row, seed 0)
span $29.8$--$35.7\%$ on shape and $36.1$--$41.7\%$ on dynamics: a 6-point spread against
roughly $5$ points of sampling error. We therefore report the ablation table as
\emph{inconclusive at this budget} (raw numbers in \texttt{results/ablation.jsonl}, table on the
project page) and take from it only two directional observations: the LoRA fast set does not
outperform direct updates (consistent with AdaJEPA's own ablation), and removing the safety
layer is the only change that is directionally favourable on both families.
