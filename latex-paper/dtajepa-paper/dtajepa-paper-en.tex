\begin{abstract}
\noindent Latent world models plan by rolling out a learned predictor in a compact latent
space, yet the predictor is almost always frozen after training, and its predictions silently
degrade under test-time distribution shift. AdaJEPA showed that a single self-supervised
gradient step per model-predictive-control (MPC) replan already recovers much of that loss.
We argue that this is the wrong unit of adaptation: a fixed one-step update applies the same
compute and the same plasticity whether the world model already fits the test environment or
is being asked to predict dynamics it has never seen, and it throws away everything learned at
the end of an episode.

\medskip
\noindent We introduce \textbf{DTA-JEPA}, a latent world model whose adaptation is
\emph{dual-timescale} and \emph{uncertainty-allocated}. The predictor is split into a slow
\emph{S-module} that produces a dynamics context from a short history and is never modified
online, and a fast \emph{R-module} that recursively refines the next latent through $K_t$
uncertainty-gated refinement steps with deep supervision at every depth. A single surprise
signal---the Mahalanobis prediction residual under the model's own Gaussian---allocates all
online resources: gradient steps, learning rate, refinement depth, planning conservatism,
memory priority, and rollback. Adaptation is confined to a rank-4 LoRA fast parameter set on
the R-module plus the encoder head, protected by an anchor ball and a validation-gated
rollback; slow parameters are consolidated only at episode boundaries under an EWC penalty,
and fast parameters are stored in a fingerprint-indexed skill bank that warm-starts later
episodes.

\medskip
\noindent We replicate AdaJEPA's evaluation protocol---shape, visual, dynamics and layout
shifts, with goals sampled as future frames of held-out trajectories---on a self-contained
numpy benchmark, and compare Frozen, a faithful AdaJEPA re-implementation and DTA-JEPA under an
identical planner, budget and episode set.

\medskip
\noindent The empirical result is negative and we report it as such: at equal budget the three
methods are statistically indistinguishable overall (combined $42.5$, $42.9$ and $45.2\%$,
per-condition spread $21$--$23$ points) and the meta-learned initialisation produces no
measurable gain --- but the exception is the one the design predicts: under a reachable
\emph{dynamics} shift (damping $\times5$) DTA-JEPA reaches the goal in $41.7\%$ of episodes
against $33.3\%$ for AdaJEPA and $16.7\%$ for the frozen model. We also report the three
measurements that explain why, because they constrain the design space more than the headline
comparison does: (i) recursive refinement reduces one-step latent error by $42\%$ from depth
one to depth three and \emph{degrades} at depth four; (ii) the predicted variance is
rank-informative (Spearman $0.55$--$0.64$) but scale-compressed (relative calibration error
$82\%$), so allocation on absolute thresholds saturates and only ratio-based allocation is
sound; and (iii) with $594.7$k fast parameters the anchor ball of radius $0.8$ binds on
essentially every gradient step, so the safety layer---not the learning rate---sets the
effective step size. A fourth finding is about the benchmark itself: every photometric
corruption we apply is a no-op for a latent-distance planner that sees the corruption
consistently in both the current and the goal frame (blur changes the one-step error by
$0.02\%$).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords}: latent world model; joint-embedding predictive architecture;
test-time adaptation; model predictive control; uncertainty; continual learning; recursive
reasoning
We then derive STLS-JEPA from four measured diagnostics of DTA-JEPA (thresholded allocation,
latent straightening, a calibrated trust region, curvature-gated refinement). Every mechanism-level
prediction for it is confirmed --- gradient steps per update fall from 2.32 to 1.11, the effective step
size stops saturating, recursion is spent only on non-straight trajectories and anchor projections fall
from 13.5 to 0.0 per episode --- and none of it transfers to planning success: 38.1\% against
39.9\% on the shape family and 18.8\% against 17.7\% on dynamics. Improving the world model
does not settle the question either: straightening cuts next-step error by 64\% on the maze while
halving closed-loop planning success, so one-step accuracy does not predict planning.

\end{abstract}
